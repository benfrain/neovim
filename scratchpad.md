## Dygma Sonsei

The Sonsei from Dygma is a unibody, 56 key, programmable, mechanical keyboard, with a columnar layout. It is interesting, and good, and yes, you should probably consider it. Let me tell you why.

### Background

Dygma offered to send a Sonsei for review and with it being Dygma, I knew some things would almost be a given – high quality, great support, great aesthetics but I did still have questions. Why unibody and not split? Why not choc switches? Can this take over as my 'work in the office' board, which is currently either my beloved Corne-ish Zen or the bigger, but no less excellent Go60 from MoErgo? And crucially, does it look more like a huge Batarang? Or as a Reddit user wrote, and I can no longer unsee – a very skinny pair of underpants, complete with bulge?

### About Sonsei

If you have had a Dygma product before, you will know their presentation is beautiful, and it's no different with the Sonsei.

The Sonsei board itself comes inside a material 'sleeve' which is your travel case. There is a zipper pocket on the cover, with a couple of mesh pouches.

In there, you get a long and a short braided USB-C cable, key puller, and a couple of spare switches. The sleeve isn't massively protected but I think it's plenty and crucially doesn't add unneeded bulk. I've been slipping it into this Anker backpack, alongside my laptop and you can barely notice it's there. I also appreciate that it has a magnetic clasp so it's just much quicker to pull out and make use of.

It's got a nice, almost bead-blasted, matt finish I really like. It's incredibly solid, made from aliminium and got these very low, like 1mm feet on the bottom that keep it in place perfectly. The battery is large 2000MaH, and sits in the blob bit in the middle. If 2000MaH means nothing to you - know that I've used this for an entire month now over Bluetooth from a single charge, and that's using it 8 hours a day, 5 days a week. And here's a wonderful little detail – the feet have holes cut in them so if you need to get into the enclosure down the line, to replace a battery for example, you don't have to pull the feet off, just stick your M2 screwdriver in through the gap. The battery itself isn't soldered to the board inside, it's just a standard JST style push fitting affair. I love that attention to detail, and some point in the future if the battery dies, I will no doubt appreciate it all over again.

The keycaps are double shot PBT plastic, rather than laser etched or dye sublimation. PBT and especially double-shot is my personal preference, as they are never going to wear out and go shiny – the legends will go all the way through the cap like a stick of rock – shoutout to anyone who went to Blackpool as a kid. In terms of keycap size, the only slightly custom ones are the thumb switches, but it will take standard 1u keycaps.

One minor strike against the Sonsei is it doesn't have any homing keycaps for Colemak or Dvorak layout users. That said we are certainly a minority so I understand the choice. But for better touch typing I left the homing key at that spot because you rarely look at the legends in practice anyway. But it sure would have been nice if they were included. This isn't a trivial investment after all.

It uses Gateron KS-33 2.0 switches, rather than the more common Kaihl choc switches you typically find in a low profile board, and I'll talk about those shortly.

You can connect the Sonsei wired, over USB-C, or wirelessly over Bluetooth – it can connect and switch to upto 5 devices. There is a little switch on the side to toggle those modes or turn off entirely. You can charge the Sonsei by plugging in a USB-C cable when it is in any of those switch positions.

For the vast majority of my time with the board I have been using it over Bluetooth and it has not skipped a beat. I turn it on, it connects, and away I go.

You can order optional palm rests for the Sonsei, but because it is so low, I didn't feel the need to use them. The Sonsei is only XXmm off the desk and I never got the slightest sense of discomfort using it. If you are wondering whether to get the palm pads, unless you know you will definitely need them, I'd skip them and use the money for some other switches or some dash style keycaps.

### Programming with Bazecor

Dygma keyboards all use a piece of software called Bazecor to program them. It's fully programmable including the colours for each key, the timings for tap and hold and such. Pretty much anything you could reasonably want to program or tweak, you can. It's not quite as fully featured and tweakable as ZSA's ORYX which you find on the Voyager, or ZMK, which powers almost all other split wireless programmable boards. That said Bazecor has improved massively over the last couple of years, and is now at a point where I have home row mods working, even with Hyper on a key – which did not used to be the case.

One of the big appeals of Bazecor, is the auto saving of changes to the board. You change stuff, click save, and its on your board. No putting your board into bootloader mode, dragging and dropping firmware files onto USB devices or any of that. You just click Save. Very user friendly to get what you want set up, along with any colours, special key features and the like.

Here are some examples of the kind of features you can enjoy – the aforementioned Home Row Mods – I tap this key and it is a 't', if I hold it down it is a 'shift'. If I tap this one it is a 'g', if I hold it, it's a Hyper, which is the equivalent of holding down ctrl, alt, cmd and shift all at once. Very handy for app launches and such, have a look at this other video if you want some ideas there. When it comes to setting up home row mods, I prefer to do it from one interface, which is how you do it on the Go60, or Voyager, whereas in Bazecor you set up Superkeys separately, and then assign physical keys to that Superkey. But your mind my prefer it the Bazecor way, I don't think one is objectively better.

You can also do layers, where you hold or tap a certain key and every key does something different. I have one for navigation, one for a number pad. You can have more layers than you could ever realistically need.

If you have a Dygma Defy keyboard, you can port your layout to the Sonsei. To do that, you can go to GGGGGG > GGGGGGG and choose load, and choose one of your Defy backups. You will get some oddities, typically around the thumb switches but it will save you a heap of time if you have things like superkeys and multiple layers already set up.

One thing that tripped me up again is that when you first pair your board, you get asked to enter a bluetooth code, and if your numbers aren't 'normal' numbers (perhaps you have them as part of a superkey setup) then it won't work. So just a little gotcha. Probably easier to pair it to your machine when you first get it, and then do your customisations.

### But no split?

I use a Dygma Defy quite a bit as one of my daily drivers. But I tend to leave it at home when I go to the office because, even with the newer travel pouch that Dygma make, it's still pretty chunky. So when I work in the office, I use other boards like the Cornish-zen, Go60, and Voyager that are both smaller and lighter to travel with, whilst still providing the split columnar programmable board layout I'm used to and appreciate.

Now, I've also recently looked at the Elytra, which isn't a columnar design but it does have the same kind of remit in that it is designed for lightweight travel. One thing that is nice about that is that while split, it has a magnetic join in the middle so that when you have it on a desk you can treat it like a single board and save the shuffle of halves. There is nothing like that on the Sonsei.

There is a double-edged sword when it comes to split keyboards. The best thing is the comfort afforded by moving the halves away from each other. It's hard to give that up once you have enjoyed it. The flip-side of that is that you can feel you never have the halves in exactly the right spot relative to one another, and you find yourself doing little OCD style nudges of the halves to try and get things perfectly aligned. A split is also inherently problematic if you want to use it in a confined space, like on your lap, or the need that Dygma themselves highlight, on a train or plane.

No question about it, for such a scenario, Dygma made the correct design choice. But if you are a split enthusiast, can you get on with the Sonsei?

Here's the thing. On the wall behind me are a couple of 'Boardwalk' layout boards. I have another one using the similar Promenade PCB board here too. Suffice to say, I'm a fan of columnar layout non-split boards, even mono-block ones. I just keep coming back to them. I'd also venture that because I'm not a particularly accurate typer, the predictability of knowing where one hand is in relation to the other helps me a little accuracy wise – but that's a fine margin thing.

So for me, I've actually loved using the Sonsei. I took to it straight away, despite being very comfortable and happy using splits like the Voyager, Go60, Defy and Advantage360 ordinarily. The transition from split to non-split is easier and preferable than the transition from staggered to non-staggered layout.

## Typing feel

So, this one has got the Gateron KS33 2.0 switches, the Strawberry Chocolate linear variants. The KS33 switches have the same footprint as MX switches, at 19mm by 19mm, as opposed to the Choc's tighter 18x17mm. I've said before on other reviews, when I'm typing, I really can't sense any difference in the spacing between Choc and MX and it's the same here with the KS switches.

The Strawberry Chocolates are not the quietest but I really like them. Because of the construction of the Sonsei, and the lengths they have gone to to insulate and dampen the chassis, there is a wonderfully satisfying clicky-clacky feel.

The KS33 can use standard MX keycaps because it has the same cross pattern but because it is lower profile, normal keycaps will likely bottom out here, and besides - these are really nice keycaps so I'm not sure why you would want to swap them out, other than getting a set of their 'Dash' ones, that would probably suit me better because I don't use QWERTY.

So look, I'm a little short of things to tell you about the typing experience other than it's really good, I like the sound and feel and I have nothing negative to tell you. I found I was able to adapt the standard layout I use on my other boards pretty simply and I've just got on and used it.

## No trackpad or trackball

Recently, I've been asked to review a few keyboards that include trackpads or trackballs and I have declined. They have been good looking boards but I just don't get on with trackpads - I don't even use the one on MacBooks. So I haven't felt qualified to evaluate the merits, or not, of any built in trackpads or trackballs. So, the fact the Sonsei has no pointer device built in is not a negative to me, but it may be a concern for you. If you are after a great board, that is a good travel option, and includes trackpads, the MoErgo Go60 would get my vote. Personally, even though I use that board quite a bit and like it, I would be just as happy if it didn't have trackpads because I never touch them.

## Conclusion

None of these niche columnar ergonomic keyboards are cheap. So, you almost just have to accept that's the game you are in. Compared to its peers it's build quality, programmability and software make it as good value as any of its competitors.

It has a 2 year warranty, and 30-day returns policy. And the support on the Dygma Discord server is always excellent. I've muddled myself up with a few things over the years when using their boards and they have always been very prompt to point me in the right direction.

This is a great piece of kit, and it absolutely nailed it's mission.

It's compact, beautifully made, feels great to type on and has been utterly reliable for the two months I've been using it, and I've been using it almost everyday.

I have no hesitation in recommending it to you if you are after a really solid column layout travel board.
